Athletic trainer jobs exist across a wider landscape than most certified ATs realize. Beyond the traditional settings of university athletics, high school sidelines, and outpatient clinics, a large and growing sector of the profession operates inside the United States military — serving active-duty service members at installations across the country and around the world.

Military healthcare has become one of the most significant sources of athletic trainer employment in the profession. The Army's Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F) program, the Marine Corps Sports Medicine and Injury Prevention (SMIP) program, and expanding pilot initiatives across other branches have created a career pathway that offers clinical autonomy, interdisciplinary teamwork, predictable schedules, and a mission that carries real weight.

This guide covers everything a certified athletic trainer needs to know about pursuing athletic trainer jobs in military healthcare: what programs are hiring, what the work looks like, where positions are located, what qualifications are required, how compensation is structured, and how to apply.

## Why Military Healthcare Is Hiring Athletic Trainers

The Department of Defense recognized years ago that musculoskeletal injuries were the leading cause of limited duty days and medical separations across the armed forces. A RAND Corporation study found that these injuries cost the Army alone billions of dollars annually and degraded unit readiness at scale. The response was not incremental — it was structural.

Rather than continuing to rely on reactive sick-call models, every military branch has moved toward embedding injury prevention and human performance professionals directly within operational units. Athletic trainers sit at the center of this shift. Their training in musculoskeletal evaluation, rehabilitation, injury prevention, and return-to-activity decision-making aligns precisely with what the military needs.

The result is a growing number of athletic trainer positions distributed across military installations in the continental United States, Hawaii, Alaska, Germany, Japan, South Korea, and other locations. These are not temporary assignments. They are contract-funded positions with defined schedules, competitive compensation, and long-term stability.

## The Two Major Military AT Programs

Two programs account for the majority of athletic trainer jobs in military healthcare today. Understanding the distinction between them is essential for any AT exploring this career path.

### Army Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F)

The H2F program is the Army's flagship investment in soldier readiness and performance. It embeds multidisciplinary performance teams — including athletic trainers, strength and conditioning coaches, physical therapists, occupational therapists, registered dietitians, and cognitive performance specialists — directly within brigade combat teams and other operational units.

H2F is the largest employer of athletic trainers in military healthcare. The program operates at more than 25 installations and continues to expand, with a Congressional mandate to embed performance teams across all active-duty brigades. Each H2F team is assigned to a specific unit, meaning athletic trainers build ongoing clinical relationships with the service members they serve rather than cycling through episodic encounters.

Athletic trainers in H2F function as the primary musculoskeletal care providers for their assigned units. They evaluate injuries, design rehabilitation protocols, manage return-to-duty timelines, and coordinate with the broader interdisciplinary team. The clinical autonomy in this role often exceeds what ATs experience in traditional settings.

### Marine Corps Sports Medicine and Injury Prevention (SMIP)

The SMIP program serves a parallel function within the Marine Corps. It embeds athletic trainers and other sports medicine professionals at Marine Corps installations to reduce musculoskeletal injury rates and improve Marine readiness.

SMIP positions are concentrated at major Marine Corps bases, with a focus on supporting the unique physical demands Marines face — from infantry training to amphibious operations. The program has expanded steadily and continues to add positions as the Marine Corps invests in injury prevention infrastructure.

While the organizational structure differs from H2F, the clinical work is similar: musculoskeletal evaluation, treatment, rehabilitation, injury prevention programming, and integration with broader medical and performance teams.

### Other Programs and Emerging Opportunities

Beyond H2F and SMIP, athletic trainers serve in other military healthcare roles:

- **Preservation of the Force and Family (POTFF):** U.S. Special Operations Command embeds athletic trainers within special operations units to support elite operator readiness.
- **Air Force and Navy pilot programs:** Both branches have begun exploring expanded roles for athletic trainers in operational and training environments.
- **Military Treatment Facilities (MTFs):** Some athletic trainers work within installation hospitals and clinics, providing outpatient musculoskeletal care.

The trajectory across all branches points in one direction: more athletic trainer positions, embedded closer to the operational force, with greater clinical responsibility.

## What Military Athletic Trainer Jobs Actually Look Like

The daily experience of an athletic trainer in military healthcare differs from traditional settings in meaningful ways. Understanding these differences helps candidates determine whether this career path aligns with their professional goals.

### Daily Schedule

One of the most significant quality-of-life advantages of military AT positions is the schedule. Athletic trainers in these programs typically work 40-hour weeks, Monday through Friday, with a standard shift of approximately daytime hours There are 11 federal holidays off per year, and overtime is rare.

Compare this to the 50- to 60-hour weeks, weekend coverage, and evening event obligations that characterize many collegiate and high school athletic training positions. In military healthcare, athletic trainers go home every night, have their weekends free, and can plan their personal lives with predictability.

### The Interdisciplinary Team

Military AT positions are team-based roles. Rather than working as a solo provider covering events and managing an entire athletic department's musculoskeletal needs alone, athletic trainers in H2F and SMIP work alongside physical therapists, strength and conditioning coaches, dietitians, occupational therapists, and cognitive performance specialists.

This structure means clinical decision-making is collaborative. Athletic trainers bring their musculoskeletal expertise to the team while benefiting from the perspectives and skills of other disciplines. It also means the workload is distributed — no single provider carries the burden alone.

### Patient Population

The service members athletic trainers treat in military healthcare represent a distinct patient population. Unlike college athletes who participate in defined sports with predictable movement patterns, soldiers and Marines perform a wide range of physical tasks: rucking with heavy loads, obstacle courses, combatives, airborne operations, multi-day field exercises, and the physical demands of their specific military occupational specialties.

This diversity of physical demands means athletic trainers encounter a broad spectrum of injuries and conditions. The clinical variety keeps the work engaging and continuously develops an AT's diagnostic and treatment skills.

### Clinical Autonomy

Athletic trainers in military healthcare programs often operate with greater clinical autonomy than their counterparts in traditional settings. As the primary musculoskeletal provider for a unit, the AT conducts evaluations, makes treatment decisions, manages rehabilitation timelines, and determines return-to-duty readiness. This level of responsibility is a significant professional draw for athletic trainers who want to practice at the top of their credential.

### Mission and Purpose

The work carries a dimension that differs from keeping an athlete in a game or on the field. Service members deploy to real-world operations. The athletic trainer's contribution to their health and readiness has consequences that extend beyond a season record. Many ATs who transition into military healthcare report that this sense of mission and purpose is one of the most fulfilling aspects of the role.

Athletic trainer positions in military healthcare span a wide geographic footprint. Major installation clusters include:

- **Fort Cavazos, Texas** — III Corps headquarters. Major H2F site in central Texas.

### Overseas

- **Camp Humphreys, South Korea** — Growing H2F presence on the Korean peninsula.

- **Okinawa, Japan** — SMIP positions supporting Marine Corps units in the Pacific.

New locations continue to open as both H2F and SMIP expand. The geographic diversity means athletic trainers can find positions that match their lifestyle preferences, whether they want to stay close to home or pursue an assignment overseas.

## Qualifications and Requirements

Athletic trainer jobs in military healthcare require a specific set of credentials and clearances. The core requirements include:

### Education and Certification

- **Master's degree** in athletic training or a related field from a CAATE-accredited program
- **BOC certification** — Active, unrestricted Board of Certification credential
- **State licensure** — Current AT license in the state where the installation is located (or ability to obtain one prior to start)

### Experience

Most positions require a minimum of one to two years of clinical experience post-certification. Some roles, particularly lead or senior positions, require three to five years or more. Experience with tactical or military populations is valued but not always required.

### Certifications

- **BLS certification** (Basic Life Support) — Required for all positions
- **AED certification** — Typically required alongside BLS
- Additional certifications such as CSCS, CES, or specialized manual therapy credentials can strengthen an application

### Security Clearance

All positions on military installations require a background investigation. Most athletic trainer positions require a favorable National Agency Check with Inquiries (NACI) or equivalent. Candidates must be U.S. citizens or permanent residents who can pass a federal background check.

### Physical Requirements

The work involves physical activity: lifting, bending, standing for extended periods, and occasionally working in field training environments. Candidates should be able to meet the physical demands of an active clinical practice.

## Compensation and Benefits

Athletic trainer jobs in military healthcare through government contractors like Cognito Systems, a joint venture of Resolution Think and QuarterLine, staffed by PSI, offer competitive compensation packages that compare favorably to traditional AT settings.

### Base Pay

Compensation is competitive with or above market rates for the athletic training profession. Specific figures vary by location — installations in higher cost-of-living areas or overseas locations may carry additional allowances. Rather than a one-size-fits-all salary band, military AT compensation reflects geographic and mission-specific factors.

### Benefits Package

The total compensation package extends well beyond base pay:

- **Health, dental, and vision insurance** — Comprehensive coverage for employees and eligible dependents
- **Paid time off** — Generous PTO plus 11 federal holidays annually
- **401(k) retirement plan** — With employer matching contributions
- **Relocation assistance** — Available for candidates moving to accept a position
- **Housing allowances** — For overseas and certain CONUS assignments
- **Continuing education support** — Assistance with BOC continuing education requirements and professional development
- **Life insurance and disability coverage**

### Total Compensation Perspective

When evaluating military AT compensation, consider the full package. The combination of competitive base pay, comprehensive benefits, paid holidays, predictable 40-hour weeks, and relocation support often results in a total compensation package that exceeds what many traditional AT roles offer — particularly when factoring in the work-life balance advantage of not working nights, weekends, or holidays.

## Who Is Hiring: Cognito Systems and PSI

The majority of athletic trainer positions in H2F and SMIP are filled through government contractors. Cognito Systems is the prime contractor for a significant portion of these programs. Cognito Systems operates as a joint venture of Resolution Think and QuarterLine, with staffing managed by PSI (Planned Systems International).

As the staffing arm of Cognito Systems, PSI recruits, hires, and supports athletic trainers across the H2F and SMIP footprint. This means that when an athletic trainer accepts a position through PSI, they become a civilian employee of the contractor — not a government employee and not active-duty military. They work on military installations alongside service members and military medical staff, but their employment relationship is with PSI.

This contractor model offers several advantages:

- **Civilian employee status** — No military obligation, no deployments, no PCS moves
- **Defined work schedules** — Contract terms specify duty hours and workload expectations
- **Professional support** — PSI provides onboarding, credentialing assistance, and ongoing HR support
- **Career mobility** — Athletic trainers can transfer between installations as positions open

### Step 2: Prepare Your Application

### Step 3: Submit and Interview

After submitting your application, qualified candidates are contacted for interviews. The interview process evaluates both clinical competence and cultural fit for the military healthcare environment. Hiring managers look for athletic trainers who are clinically strong, team-oriented, and aligned with the mission of supporting service member readiness.

### Step 4: Background Check and Onboarding

Selected candidates undergo a federal background investigation. Once cleared, PSI manages the onboarding process, including credentialing, relocation coordination, and orientation at the assigned installation.

The timeline from application to start date can vary depending on the background check process and position availability, but PSI works to move candidates through the pipeline efficiently.

## Is Military Healthcare the Right Fit?

Military athletic trainer jobs are not for every AT. The work environment is structured, the patient population is specific, and the installation setting is different from a university campus or outpatient clinic.

But for athletic trainers who value clinical autonomy, interdisciplinary collaboration, a predictable 40-hour schedule, competitive total compensation, and work that carries genuine mission impact, military healthcare represents one of the strongest career paths in the profession today.

The demand for qualified athletic trainers in these programs continues to outpace supply. Every branch of the military is moving toward greater investment in human performance and injury prevention. The athletic trainers who enter this space now are positioning themselves at the front of a career pathway that is growing, not contracting.
