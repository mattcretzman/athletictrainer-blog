## The Athletic Trainer Career Outlook Has Never Been Stronger

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 14% job growth for athletic trainers through 2032 — nearly three times the average for all occupations. That number alone should get the attention of anyone considering the profession or looking to make a career move within it.

But the headline number only tells part of the story. Where that growth is happening, and which sectors are driving it, matters just as much as the growth rate itself. For athletic trainers paying attention, one sector stands out above the rest: military healthcare.

The U.S. Army's Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F) program is expanding to every brigade combat team across the force. The Marine Corps' Semper Fit Martial Arts and Injury Prevention (SMIP) program continues to grow its footprint. The Air Force and Navy are launching their own pilot programs built around human performance optimization. Every branch of the military is moving toward integrating athletic trainers into its healthcare and readiness infrastructure.

This is not a temporary initiative or a pilot that may get defunded. It is a structural shift in how the Department of Defense approaches service member health, performance, and injury prevention. And it is creating a sustained demand for credentialed athletic trainers that the profession has not seen before.

## Traditional AT Roles Are Saturated

To understand why the military healthcare sector matters so much to the athletic trainer career outlook, it helps to understand the landscape athletic trainers currently face.

There are roughly 50,000 certified athletic trainers in the United States. The vast majority of them are competing for positions in a handful of traditional settings: secondary schools, colleges and universities, outpatient clinics, and professional sports organizations.

### The Secondary School Bottleneck

Secondary schools remain the single largest employer of athletic trainers. But the positions are often underfunded, understaffed, and structurally demanding. A single athletic trainer may be responsible for covering every sport at a school — sometimes across multiple campuses. The National Athletic Trainers' Association has long advocated for better ratios, but progress has been slow. In many states, one athletic trainer covers upward of 2,700 student-athletes.

The result is a setting where athletic trainers are stretched thin, working evenings and weekends as a matter of course, and often supplementing their income with other roles because compensation does not reflect the scope of responsibility.

### Collegiate and Professional Sports

Collegiate athletic training positions are highly competitive, with limited openings and significant turnover pressure tied to coaching changes and conference realignment. Professional sports organizations employ a relatively small number of athletic trainers nationwide, and those positions rarely open up.

### Clinical and Outpatient Settings

The clinical sector has grown in recent years, but it comes with its own constraints. Athletic trainers in outpatient settings often function under physician extender models, with productivity expectations that can feel more like a billing operation than a clinical practice. The work is steady, but the ceiling for professional growth can be low.

### The Common Thread

Across all of these traditional settings, the common thread is the same: there are more qualified athletic trainers than there are quality positions. Competition is high, compensation has been slow to rise, and the conditions of employment — long hours, weekend obligations, solo coverage models — have remained largely unchanged for decades.

That is the context that makes the military healthcare sector so significant.

## Why Military Healthcare Is Different

The Department of Defense is not adding athletic trainers as an afterthought or a nice-to-have. The military is building entire performance systems around the athletic training skillset, and it is doing so at a scale that dwarfs anything happening in the civilian sector.

### The H2F Expansion

The Army's H2F program is the most visible driver of this growth. Originally piloted with a handful of brigade combat teams, the program has been expanding steadily and is on track to reach every brigade across the active-duty force. Each H2F team includes athletic trainers as core members alongside strength and conditioning coaches, physical therapists, dietitians, and cognitive performance specialists.

This is not a model where one athletic trainer is responsible for an entire installation. H2F teams are built with depth and redundancy. Athletic trainers work alongside other credentialed professionals in a genuinely interdisciplinary environment.

The scale of the expansion is significant. As H2F reaches full implementation, the demand for qualified athletic trainers across Army installations — from Fort Liberty in North Carolina to Fort Cavazos in Texas to installations in Hawaii, Japan, Germany, and beyond — represents a growing number of positions that simply did not exist a few years ago.

### SMIP and the Marine Corps

The Marine Corps has been building its own approach through the SMIP program, which integrates injury prevention and human performance principles into the training culture of Marine units. Athletic trainers play a central role in this effort, providing the injury surveillance, movement screening, and rehabilitation oversight that the program depends on.

As the Marine Corps continues to invest in force readiness and injury reduction, the demand for athletic trainers within SMIP is growing in parallel with the Army's H2F expansion.

### Air Force and Navy Pilot Programs

The Air Force and Navy are earlier in their adoption curves, but both branches are launching pilot programs that incorporate athletic trainers and human performance professionals into their operational health models. These programs are drawing directly on lessons learned from the Army's H2F rollout and the Marine Corps' SMIP experience.

The trajectory is clear: every branch of the U.S. military is moving toward a model that requires athletic trainers. The only variable is how quickly each branch scales.

## The Supply-Demand Imbalance

Here is the core dynamic that makes the athletic trainer career outlook in military healthcare so compelling: demand is outpacing supply.

The number of athletic training programs producing new graduates each year has not changed dramatically. Meanwhile, the military healthcare sector is adding positions at a rate that the profession's pipeline was not designed to fill. This is happening at the same time that traditional settings continue to absorb graduates, meaning the pool of available candidates for military positions is smaller than the demand would require.

### What This Means for Athletic Trainers

For athletic trainers currently in the workforce, this supply-demand imbalance creates genuine leverage. Positions in military healthcare offer competitive compensation, federal benefits, and working conditions that stand in sharp contrast to the norms of traditional AT employment.

Consider the structural differences:

- **Scheduled hours.** Military healthcare athletic trainers typically work 40-hour weeks, Monday through Friday, with standard hours of 6:30 a.m. to 2:30 p.m. No evening games. No weekend tournaments. No on-call coverage.

- **Team-based care.** Instead of functioning as the sole provider, athletic trainers in military healthcare work within multidisciplinary teams. The isolation that defines so many traditional AT roles is replaced by daily collaboration with other healthcare and performance professionals.

- **Professional development.** The military healthcare environment supports continuing education and professional growth in ways that many traditional settings cannot match. Access to advanced equipment, exposure to high-level injury prevention research, and collaboration with specialists across disciplines are built into the role.

- **Stability.** These positions are tied to Department of Defense contracts and programs with long-term Congressional funding support. The demand is structural, not cyclical.

## The Career Trajectory for Military Healthcare ATs

One aspect of the athletic trainer career outlook that deserves more attention is the career trajectory available within military healthcare. This is not a dead-end role. Athletic trainers who enter the military healthcare sector through programs like H2F and SMIP can move into supervisory positions, program management roles, and leadership tracks within the human performance ecosystem.

### From Clinician to Program Leader

The H2F model, in particular, creates clear pathways from direct clinical care to program-level leadership. As the program expands, it needs not only front-line athletic trainers but also site leads, regional coordinators, and program managers who understand the clinical work because they have done it.

Athletic trainers who build experience in military healthcare are positioned to grow into these roles in a way that is rarely available in traditional settings. In a secondary school, the career ceiling for an athletic trainer is often the same position held for 20 years. In military healthcare, the expanding program creates upward mobility as a natural consequence of growth.

### Cross-Disciplinary Expertise

Working within a multidisciplinary team also develops skills and expertise that have value beyond any single role. Athletic trainers in military healthcare gain experience in population health management, data-driven injury prevention, return-to-duty protocols, and performance optimization at scale. These competencies are increasingly valued across the broader healthcare and human performance landscape.

## What This Means for the Profession

The military healthcare sector is not just good news for individual athletic trainers looking for better jobs. It represents a meaningful shift in how the profession is perceived and utilized.

For decades, athletic training has struggled with an identity challenge. The general public, and even many healthcare administrators, have had a limited understanding of what athletic trainers do and the level of education and credentialing the profession requires. Military healthcare is changing that narrative by placing athletic trainers at the center of high-profile, well-funded performance and healthcare programs.

When the Department of Defense builds entire readiness programs around the athletic training skillset, it sends a signal to every other sector about the value of the profession. That signal has downstream effects on compensation, professional recognition, and scope of practice discussions that benefit all athletic trainers, regardless of where they work.

## The Numbers Behind the Outlook

The 14% growth projection from the BLS is an aggregate number. It includes growth across all settings — schools, clinics, hospitals, and the emerging military healthcare sector. But the distribution of that growth is not even.

Traditional settings are growing modestly at best. Some are contracting as schools consolidate and clinics optimize staffing models. The outsized growth is concentrated in newer sectors, and military healthcare is the largest and most consistent of those sectors.

For athletic trainers making career decisions in 2026 and beyond, the question is not whether the profession is growing. It is. The question is where the growth is happening and whether the positions being created align with the kind of career — and the kind of life — an athletic trainer wants to build.

## The Bottom Line on the Athletic Trainer Career Outlook

The athletic trainer career outlook is strong by any measure. But for those willing to look beyond the traditional settings that have defined the profession for decades, the military healthcare sector offers something more than just job growth. It offers a fundamentally different model of employment — one built around reasonable hours, team-based care, professional development, and long-term stability.

The H2F program is expanding. SMIP is growing. The Air Force and Navy are launching new initiatives. Every branch of the military is investing in athletic trainers. The demand is real, it is growing, and it is outpacing the supply of qualified candidates.

For athletic trainers who want to build a career in a setting that values their skills, respects their time, and offers a clear path for growth, military healthcare is not just an option. It is the growth sector.
