## Introduction: Two Paths, One Profession


The athletic training profession is expanding well beyond the sidelines. While sports settings remain the most visible career path, a parallel track has emerged within the Department of Defense — one that offers competitive compensation, meaningful work, and a fundamentally different daily rhythm. Understanding the full picture of **military vs sports athletic training** is essential for any certified athletic trainer weighing long-term career options.


### What Certified Athletic Trainers Actually Do


Athletic trainers are licensed healthcare professionals who specialize in the prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation of musculoskeletal injuries. Whether you work with a college football team or a brigade combat team, the clinical foundation is the same: evidence-based assessment, therapeutic intervention, emergency care, and return-to-activity decision-making. The Board of Certification (BOC) credential and state licensure underpin both career paths.


What differs is the patient population, the operational context, and the professional ecosystem surrounding your work.


### Why Military Athletic Training Is a Growing Career Path


The Department of Defense has made musculoskeletal readiness a strategic priority. Approximately 25 percent of all military medical encounters involve musculoskeletal injuries, making athletic trainers a critical component of force readiness. The Army's Holistic Health and Fitness (H2F) system, launched in 2020, created a growing number of new positions for athletic trainers, physical therapists, and strength and conditioning coaches embedded directly in brigade combat teams. Across Army and Marine Corps installations, Injury Prevention Programs (IPP) and Human Performance Optimization (HPO) teams continue to expand.


The result: a career path that many athletic training students and early-career professionals have never been taught to consider.


### Who This Comparison Is For


This guide is designed for BOC-certified athletic trainers and athletic training students who are evaluating their next career move. Whether you are currently working in sports and wondering if a change of setting could improve your quality of life, or you are a student exploring all available paths before entering the workforce, this comparison provides the data and context to make an informed decision.

## Overview of Sports Athletic Training Careers


### Common Work Settings: High School, College, and Professional Sports


Sports athletic training spans a wide range of settings. At the high school level, you may be the sole healthcare provider covering multiple teams. In the collegiate setting, you typically work within an established sports medicine staff, with your caseload defined by the sport or sports you cover. At the professional level, resources increase significantly, but so do expectations and the intensity of the competitive calendar.


Each setting carries its own culture, schedule, and compensation structure. Across all of them, the work is deeply tied to the athletic season — and the athletes themselves.


### Typical Daily Responsibilities in Sports AT


A day in sports athletic training revolves around the competition and practice schedule. Morning hours may include rehabilitation sessions, pre-practice treatments, and medical documentation. Afternoons and evenings are typically consumed by practice coverage, injury evaluation, and post-practice care. Game days extend hours further, often including travel to away competitions.


Your clinical skill set is exercised broadly: acute injury assessment, therapeutic modality application, manual therapy, exercise prescription, concussion management, and communication with physicians and coaching staff.


### Career Growth and Advancement in Sports Settings


Advancement in sports athletic training generally follows a progression from assistant or seasonal positions to head athletic trainer roles. At the collegiate level, this may eventually lead to an associate or assistant athletics director for sports medicine title. In professional sports, career growth often means moving between organizations to secure a head athletic trainer position with a team.


Lateral movement between sports, levels of play, and institutions is common. However, upward mobility can be constrained by the relatively small number of leadership positions within any single organization.

## Overview of Military Athletic Training Careers


### What Does a Military Athletic Trainer Do?


A military athletic trainer provides the same core clinical services as a sports-setting counterpart — injury evaluation, rehabilitation, therapeutic intervention, and return-to-duty planning — but within the context of military healthcare. Your patients are service members: soldiers and Marines whose physical readiness directly impacts national defense.


Daily duties may include conducting injury prevention screenings for incoming units, managing rehabilitation programs for musculoskeletal injuries, collaborating with physical therapists and orthopedic surgeons in military treatment facilities, and educating service members on movement optimization and injury risk reduction. Most H2F AT work hours are 7:30 AM to 3:30 PM, give or take depending on base location.


### Working with Tactical Athletes: Soldiers and Marines


The concept of the "tactical athlete" reframes military service members as individuals whose occupational demands parallel those of competitive athletes — sustained physical output, high-impact loading, environmental stress, and the need to perform under fatigue and pressure. The difference is that the stakes extend beyond wins and losses.


For athletic trainers, this population presents a compelling clinical challenge. You may address overuse injuries from ruck marching, shoulder pathology from load carriage, lower extremity injuries from airborne operations, or the cumulative effects of years of high-intensity training. The breadth of musculoskeletal presentations mirrors what you would encounter across multiple sports — compressed into a single patient population.


### The Role of Government Contractors Like PSI in Military AT Placement


Most athletic trainers working in military settings are employed through government contracts rather than enlisting in the armed forces. ### The Army H2F System and DOD Human Performance Optimization Teams


The Army's H2F system represents the largest institutional investment in embedded athletic training within the military. H2F teams are assigned to brigade combat teams and include athletic trainers, physical therapists, strength and conditioning coaches, dietitians, and cognitive performance specialists. The athletic trainer's role within H2F is focused on injury prevention, musculoskeletal screening, acute care, and rehabilitation — all delivered at the unit level rather than in a clinic.


The Marine Corps operates similar performance optimization programs focused on tactical athlete readiness. Each creates demand for BOC-certified athletic trainers who understand the tactical environment. These positions require experienced, BOC-certified athletic trainers with professional clinical experience.

## Salary and Benefits: Military vs. Sports Athletic Training


### Civilian Sports AT Salary Ranges by Setting


According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, athletic trainer compensation varies significantly by employer type and setting:


- **High school athletic trainers** often earn salaries that vary by geographic location and district funding.
- **Collegiate athletic trainers** earn salaries that vary by division level and institution, with Division I head athletic trainer positions reaching higher.
- **Professional sports athletic trainers** can earn significantly more, though these positions represent a very small percentage of the profession.


Benefits in sports settings vary widely. High school positions may offer school district benefits. Collegiate roles generally include university benefits packages. Professional sports benefits depend on the organization.

## Work-Life Balance and Schedule Demands


### The Seasonal Grind of Sports Athletic Training


Work-life balance remains one of the most discussed challenges in sports athletic training. During the competitive season, 50- to 70-hour weeks are common across all levels. Weekend coverage, evening games, holiday tournaments, and postseason play can consume months of the year with minimal time off.


For athletic trainers covering multiple sports — a frequent reality at the high school and small-college levels — there may be no true "off-season." This schedule can strain personal relationships, limit involvement in family life, and contribute to the burnout that the profession has increasingly acknowledged.


### Predictable Hours and Stability in Military Settings


Military athletic training positions typically operate on a more standard schedule. Most positions involve 40-hour work weeks aligned with the military installation's operating hours, generally 7:30 AM to 3:30 PM depending on base location. While occasional early mornings or field training exercises may require flexibility, the baseline schedule is predictable in a way that many sports settings are not.


This predictability matters. It enables you to plan childcare, pursue continuing education, maintain personal fitness, and sustain the kind of work-life balance that supports a long career.


<Callout type="tip">
If work-life balance is a primary factor in your career decision, ask specific questions during the interview process about typical weekly hours, field training exercise frequency, and expectations for coverage outside standard duty hours. Military positions vary by installation and unit, and understanding the specific role's rhythm will help you assess fit.
</Callout>


### Travel Requirements in Both Career Paths


Sports athletic training often involves significant travel, particularly at the collegiate Division I and professional levels. Road games, tournament travel, and conference events can account for weeks away from home each year.


Military athletic training positions are generally based at a single installation. While some roles may involve temporary travel for training exercises or conferences, the baseline expectation is that your daily work happens at your assigned location. For athletic trainers seeking geographic stability, this is a meaningful distinction. Overseas assignments at installations in Japan offer the opportunity to live and work abroad for an extended period.

## Scope of Practice and Professional Development


### Clinical Skills Used in Sports vs. Military AT Roles


Both settings draw on the full scope of athletic training practice, including injury evaluation, manual therapy, therapeutic exercise, emergency care, and patient education. Military settings may place additional emphasis on ergonomic assessment, load carriage biomechanics, and occupational injury patterns unique to military tasks. Sports settings often emphasize sport-specific rehabilitation progressions and return-to-play protocols driven by competitive timelines.


### Interdisciplinary Collaboration in Military Healthcare Teams


One of the distinguishing features of military athletic training is the depth of interdisciplinary collaboration. Within H2F and HPO teams, you may work daily alongside physical therapists, orthopedic surgeons, dietitians, psychologists, strength and conditioning coaches, and occupational therapists. This team-based model provides clinical mentorship, broadens your perspective, and creates opportunities to contribute to patient care in ways that may not be available in settings where you are the sole healthcare provider.


### Continuing Education and Research Opportunities


Military athletic training positions often provide access to continuing education funding and opportunities. Some positions involve contributing to Department of Defense research initiatives on injury prevention, biomechanics, or human performance optimization. For athletic trainers interested in evidence-based practice and clinical research, this represents a professional development pathway rarely available in traditional sports settings.


### Credentialing Differences: Civilian Licensure vs. Military Facility Privileges


Both pathways require BOC certification, state licensure, and US citizenship. However, military positions may involve additional credentialing steps. Depending on the installation, you may need to obtain clinical privileges through the military treatment facility, complete training in military electronic health record systems such as MHS GENESIS, and undergo a background investigation.


These requirements are managed with support from the contracting organization. PSI guides athletic trainers through military-specific credentialing to ensure a smooth transition into the clinical environment.

## Job Security and Market Demand


### Employment Outlook for Sports Athletic Trainers


The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects athletic trainer employment to grow 14 percent from 2022 to 2032, significantly faster than the average for all occupations. This growth spans settings, but competition for positions in professional sports and Division I collegiate athletics remains high. Many entry-level sports positions are part-time, seasonal, or carry one-year contracts tied to coaching staff tenure.


### Growing DOD Investment in Musculoskeletal Readiness


The Department of Defense's sustained investment in musculoskeletal readiness has created a growing market for athletic trainers in military settings. The expansion of H2F across the Army, combined with similar initiatives in the Marine Corps, has generated demand that exceeds the current supply of qualified candidates. This supply-demand dynamic creates favorable conditions for athletic trainers seeking military positions.


### Contract Stability and Renewal in Military Positions


Government contracts for healthcare staffing are typically awarded for multi-year periods, often with option years that extend the total contract length. While contract transitions can occur, experienced athletic trainers who perform well are consistently in demand. ## How to Transition from Sports AT to Military AT (and Vice Versa)


### Transferable Skills Between Both Settings


The clinical foundation of athletic training translates directly between sports and military environments. If you are considering a transition, recognize that you already possess many of the skills that military settings value:


- Musculoskeletal injury evaluation and differential diagnosis
- Therapeutic exercise prescription and rehabilitation program design
- Manual therapy and soft tissue mobilization techniques
- Emergency care and acute injury management
- Patient education and behavior change communication
- Electronic health record documentation
- Interdisciplinary communication with physicians, coaches, and other providers
- Evidence-based clinical decision-making
- Injury surveillance and data collection
- Program development for injury prevention initiatives


The primary adjustment involves learning the military healthcare system, understanding the tactical athlete's occupational demands, and adapting to the organizational culture of a military installation.


### Steps to Land a Military Athletic Training Position


If you are considering the transition to military athletic training, the process generally involves:


1. **Confirm your credentials.** Ensure your BOC certification is current, your state licensure is active, and you are a US citizen. Some military positions require licensure in the state where the installation is located.
2. **Research available positions.** PSI maintains current listings of military athletic trainer openings.
3. **Prepare for additional credentialing.** Be ready for a background check and military treatment facility credentialing.
4. **Understand the setting.** Familiarize yourself with the H2F system, tactical athlete populations, and the Department of Defense's approach to injury prevention and human performance optimization.
5. **Apply through PSI.** Working with an established government contractor simplifies the process and ensures you have organizational support throughout onboarding.

## Which Path Is Right for You? Key Decision Factors


Military athletic training and sports athletic training both build on the same clinical foundation but differ in schedule, compensation, patient population, and career trajectory. Sports athletic training offers immersion in competitive athletics with variable hours and employer-dependent benefits, while military athletic training provides structured schedules, competitive total compensation with comprehensive benefits, and the opportunity to serve the nation's defense readiness mission alongside interdisciplinary healthcare teams.


### Passion for Sports vs. Mission-Driven Healthcare


Your professional motivation matters. If the energy of competition, the relationship with coaching staffs, and the culture of athletics drive your sense of purpose, sports athletic training may be the right fit. If you are drawn to mission-driven healthcare, interdisciplinary collaboration, and the challenge of optimizing performance in a tactical population, military athletic training offers a deeply fulfilling alternative.


These motivations are not mutually exclusive. Many athletic trainers who transition to military settings find that the intensity of working with tactical athletes satisfies the same drive that initially drew them to sports.


### Lifestyle Priorities: Stability, Travel, and Family


Consider what your life outside of work needs to look like. If geographic stability, predictable hours, and consistent time with family are priorities, military athletic training positions align well with those values. If you thrive on the travel and adrenaline of competitive sports and are willing to accept the schedule demands that come with it, sports settings may suit your current season of life.


### Long-Term Career and Retirement Planning


PSI's competitive benefits package — including retirement plans and comprehensive health insurance — creates a career planning framework that supports long-term professional growth. If you are thinking in terms of 20- to 30-year career planning, the financial architecture of military athletic training positions deserves serious consideration.
